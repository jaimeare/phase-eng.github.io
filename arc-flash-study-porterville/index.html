Arc Flash Study in Porterville

Distribution centers, citrus packing houses, and cold storage in southern Tulare County. Close enough that scheduling around your season is straightforward.

Porterville's electrical load is concentrated in two places: large-format distribution, where a single building can exceed a million square feet, and citrus — packing houses, degreening rooms, and cold storage running hard through the season and quiet the rest of the year.

Distribution centers and automation upgrades

Retail distribution buildings are electrically simple in layout and complicated in practice. Long feeder runs, hundreds of motor loads on conveyor and sortation systems, battery charging rooms, and lighting and HVAC loads spread across enormous floor plates.

The thing that changes the arc flash picture is automation. Replacing conveyor and sortation equipment with higher-throughput automated systems adds connected load, adds motor contribution to fault current, and frequently comes with a service or transformer upgrade. Facilities that have been through an automation retrofit and not re-run the study are carrying labels calculated for a system that no longer exists.

NFPA 70E Section 130.5

The arc flash risk assessment must be reviewed at intervals not exceeding five years, and sooner whenever a change to the electrical distribution system could affect the results.

Packing houses and seasonal operation

Citrus packing has an electrical profile that does not look like year-round manufacturing. Refrigeration and controlled-atmosphere storage carry large compressor loads. Packing lines run dozens of motors and drives that get reconfigured between varieties. And the facility swings from near-idle to full load on a seasonal schedule.

That schedule is an advantage for this work. The window between seasons is when equipment can be opened, panels can be accessed without fighting production, and a study can be done thoroughly rather than around a running line. Packing operations that book the work for the off-season get a better study and a cheaper one, because nothing has to be worked around.

Scheduling around your season

Our office is in Visalia, about forty minutes north. For seasonal operations that matters practically: we can plan the site walk for your window rather than ours, and come back for the panels that were running the first time without it turning into a second mobilization charge.

What every study includes

  • Short circuit study with equipment rating evaluation
  • Protective device coordination with time current curves for every panel
  • Incident energy analysis and arc flash boundaries
  • Labels meeting NFPA 70E and NEC 110.16, printed and installed
  • Report sealed by a California licensed Professional Engineer

Three things to check before you hire anyone

Ask whether a licensed P.E. Electrical Engineer seals the final report. Ask whether every panel appears in the coordination study time current curve plots, or only the main gear. And ask who performs the field data collection — missing conductor lengths and unrecorded breaker settings go straight into the incident energy result.

We send a P.E. Electrical Engineer to collect the data, with an electrician as support to open panel covers. The engineer who has to defend the model gathers its inputs.
