Arc Flash Study in Tulare

Dairy processing, cold storage, distribution, and manufacturing. Twenty minutes from our office in Visalia, which means a site walk does not have to be a two-day commitment.

Tulare County produces more milk than any county in the United States, and the processing that follows is genuinely heavy industry. Cheese, whey, butter and powder plants running continuously, with utility services, motor control centers, and distribution systems on a scale most people associate with much larger cities. Around them sit frozen food production, nut hulling and processing, feed and grain operations, metal building and packaging manufacturers, and a distribution cluster serving retailers across the western states.

Why processing plants are difficult to model

Continuous-process facilities are the hardest arc flash studies to get right, and the most expensive to get wrong.

Ammonia refrigeration means large motor loads that contribute fault current for the first few cycles. Variable frequency drives are everywhere, and how they are represented in the model changes the result. Washdown environments mean equipment gets replaced more often than the drawings get updated. And the plant runs continuously, so nothing can be de-energized for convenience — which is exactly why the incident energy numbers need to be right the first time.

The other recurring problem is expansion. A new line, a new chiller, a new service, or an upgraded transformer changes available fault current across the system. The label installed three years ago is now describing a system that no longer exists.

NEC Article 110.24

Service equipment at other than dwelling units must be field marked with the maximum available fault current and the date of the calculation — and that marking must be verified or recalculated when modifications affect it.

Digesters and on-site generation

Dairy digesters producing power from methane have added a category of electrical work that did not exist in this county fifteen years ago. Generation tied into an existing plant distribution system changes fault current contribution, requires coordination between the generator protection and the plant's existing devices, and introduces utility interconnection requirements. If your facility added generation without a study afterward, the arc flash labels predate the change.

What every study includes

  • Short circuit study with equipment rating evaluation
  • Protective device coordination with time current curves for every panel
  • Incident energy analysis and arc flash boundaries
  • Labels meeting NFPA 70E and NEC 110.16, printed and installed
  • Report sealed by a California licensed Professional Engineer

Three things to check before you hire anyone

Ask whether a licensed P.E. Electrical Engineer seals the final report. Ask whether every panel appears in the coordination study time current curve plots, or only the main gear. And ask who performs the field data collection — missing conductor lengths and unrecorded breaker settings go straight into the incident energy result.

We send a P.E. Electrical Engineer to collect the data, with an electrician as support to open panel covers. The engineer who has to defend the model gathers its inputs.
